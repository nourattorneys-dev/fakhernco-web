## Your terms are the only contract you have with your users

You will never sign anything with the people who use your app. The terms they
tap past on the way in are the entire agreement — who owes what, what you may
do with their data, what happens when a payment fails, and which court hears it
if none of that goes to plan.

Most of the terms we are asked to review were copied from a competitor, or
generated from a template written for a US or UK business. They read
convincingly. They also give a UAE company obligations under laws that do not
apply to it, while leaving out the ones that do.

## The four laws that actually govern a UAE app

Terms drafted elsewhere tend to be silent on all four.

**Federal Decree-Law No. 14 of 2023 on Trading by Modern Technological Means.**
The UAE's e-commerce law, in force since September 2023. It applies to selling
through a website, an app, a social media account, a virtual store or a
blockchain platform — whether the seller is in the UAE or selling into it. It
covers licensing, what you must disclose before a customer commits, and how
logistics and returns are handled. It also restricts charging customers extra
for paying by a particular digital method, which quietly makes a surcharge
clause in a foreign template unlawful here.

**Federal Law No. 15 of 2020 on Consumer Protection**, and its Executive
Regulations in Cabinet Decision No. 66 of 2023, in force since October 2023.
Article 34 is the one that catches people out: a condition that relieves the
supplier of its obligations is void. The broad "we exclude all liability to the
maximum extent permitted by law" clause at the bottom of most templates does not
survive contact with it. Article 5 requires prices to be displayed legibly and
completely — which governs how you present a subscription price, not merely
whether you disclose it somewhere.

**Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021, the Personal Data Protection Law**, in
force since January 2022. If you collect a name, an email, a phone number or a
device identifier, this applies. It requires a lawful basis for processing,
consent that is genuine rather than bundled into a single "I agree", and it
gives users rights over their data — access, correction, deletion, and objection
— that your privacy policy has to tell them how to exercise. Transferring data
outside the UAE, which any app on a foreign cloud does by default, is regulated
rather than assumed.

**The Electronic Transactions and Trust Services Law**, which is what makes a
tick-box agreement a contract at all. Getting the acceptance mechanism right is
what turns your terms from a page nobody reads into something enforceable.

## Why the Arabic version is not optional

UAE courts operate in Arabic. If your terms exist only in English, the version a
judge reads is a translation nobody on your side has approved — and consumer
protection rules expect terms a UAE consumer can actually understand.

We draft both versions together rather than writing in English and translating
afterwards. Translation is where meaning shifts: a liability cap that was
precise in English can become ambiguous, or say something materially different,
once rendered into Arabic by someone who is not a lawyer.

  genuinely collects, not a generic list
- **End user licence agreements** for mobile apps
- **Subscription and auto-renewal terms** — the renewal, the price change and

  and the platform sitting between them
- **Refund, return, delivery and cancellation policies**
- **Acceptable use and content moderation policies** — including user-generated
  content and who owns it
- **Data processing agreements** with your suppliers and cloud providers
- **Disclaimers** for anything that could be read as professional, medical or
  financial advice
- **Age verification and parental consent terms**, where children may use the
  product

## What a review covers

1. **Whether it binds anyone.** How acceptance is captured, whether you can
   later prove a specific user agreed to a specific version, and how you push a
   change to terms without losing enforceability.
2. **The liability clauses that survive.** Rewriting blanket exclusions into
   limits a UAE court will actually uphold, rather than ones struck out whole
   under Article 34 — leaving you with none.
3. **The privacy policy against the product.** We compare what the policy claims
   with what the app really collects, including the analytics and advertising
   SDKs nobody remembers installing. A policy that understates collection is
   worse than no policy.
4. **Payments, subscriptions and refunds** — disclosure before purchase, renewal
   notice, and a cancellation route that matches what you tell the app stores.
5. **Governing law and forum** — mainland UAE, DIFC or ADGM, and whether the
   choice you have made is one that will be respected.
6. **Intellectual property** — your ownership of the product, and your licence
   to whatever users upload into it.
7. **The Arabic** — whether the two versions say the same thing, and which
   prevails.

## Common questions

**Can I just use a terms generator?**
It will produce something that looks right. It will not know that Article 34
voids your exclusion clause, that the e-commerce law restricts your payment
surcharge, or that your Arabic version is missing entirely. Those are the three
things a UAE court will look at.

**Do I need a privacy policy if I only collect an email address?**
Yes. An email address is personal data under the PDPL. Both app stores also
require a working privacy policy URL before they will publish you.

**My company is in a free zone. Does UAE federal law still apply?**
Largely yes. The PDPL and the consumer and e-commerce laws are federal.
DIFC and ADGM have their own data protection regimes that apply on top, and
which one governs depends on where you are established and where your customers
are — it is worth settling before you draft, not after.

**We are already live without proper terms. What now?**
Publish a compliant set, and make sure existing users are asked to accept them
rather than assumed to have. We can also tell you what exposure the earlier
period leaves behind, which is worth knowing even where it cannot be undone.

**Can you review terms a developer wrote for us?**
Yes, and it is one of the most common reasons clients come to us. It costs a
fraction of the complaint or claim the clause would otherwise have caused.
