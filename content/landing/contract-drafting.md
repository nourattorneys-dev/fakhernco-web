## A contract is only worth what a UAE court will enforce

Most contract disputes we are asked to litigate were created at the drafting
stage — usually by a template downloaded from the internet, or by a document
carried over from a business in another country.

Online templates are typically drafted under English or US law. The clauses that
decide what happens when things go wrong — governing law, jurisdiction, dispute
resolution, termination, penalties, limitation of liability — are often
unenforceable or simply meaningless under UAE law. The document reads
reassuringly right up until the moment you need it, which is the worst possible
time to discover it does not work.

## Drafted in Arabic and English, together

UAE courts operate primarily in Arabic. In a dispute, the Arabic version is the
one that governs — so if your contract exists only in English, the version a
judge reads will be a translation nobody on your side has approved.

We draft bilingually rather than writing in English and translating afterwards.
That distinction matters more than it sounds: translation is where meaning
quietly shifts, and a term that was precise in English can become ambiguous, or
mean something materially different, once it is rendered into Arabic by someone
who is not a lawyer.

## What a review actually covers

Sending us a contract to review is often the highest-value work we do — it costs
a fraction of the dispute the clause would otherwise have caused. We look at:

1. **Enforceability under UAE law** — whether the clauses do what they claim to
   do in this jurisdiction, rather than in the one the template came from.
2. **Governing law and forum** — which law applies, which court or tribunal hears
   it, and whether that choice is one a UAE court will respect.
3. **The exit** — how the agreement ends, who can end it, on what notice, and
   what survives termination.
4. **Money** — payment terms, interest on late payment, penalties, and whether a
   liquidated damages clause is set at a level a court will uphold.
5. **Risk allocation** — indemnities, liability caps, insurance, and force
   majeure.
6. **The Arabic** — whether the two language versions actually say the same
   thing, and which one prevails.

You get the marked-up document plus a plain-language note on the clauses that
carry real financial exposure — not just what is wrong, but what it would cost
you if it were tested.

## Common questions

**Why can't I use a template I found online?**
Because it was almost certainly drafted under another country's law. Governing
law, dispute resolution and termination clauses are the ones that matter when a
deal goes wrong, and those are exactly the ones that do not transfer. A template
gives you the feeling of protection without the substance.

**Are non-compete clauses enforceable in the UAE?**
Yes, but only if drafted correctly. UAE courts will enforce a non-compete that is
reasonable in scope — limited to a defined geographic area, a defined period, and
a genuine business interest. Clauses drafted too broadly, in the belief that
asking for more protects you better, risk being struck down entirely, which
leaves you with none.

**Can you review a contract the other side has sent me?**
Yes, and this is one of the most common reasons clients come to us. It is far
cheaper than the dispute the clause would have caused.

**Do I really need the Arabic version?**
If there is any prospect of enforcing the contract in a UAE court, yes.

**We already signed. Is it too late?**
Not necessarily. There may be scope to vary the agreement, and knowing where you
are exposed is worth having even if the terms cannot be changed — it tells you
what to avoid doing.
